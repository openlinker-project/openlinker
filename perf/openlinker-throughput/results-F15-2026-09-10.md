## 1. What this measures

- **arm A** - label issuance throughput through `ShipmentDispatchService`
  against `perf-shipping` (shipping-stub) at a declared 500 ms latency.
- **arm B** - the late-waybill relay (#1947). A carrier that mints the tracking
  number AFTER the operator dispatched: the arm asserts the `null -> value`
  transition relays a second `dispatched` event and stamps
  `Shipment.waybillRelayedAt`.
- **arm C** - the same relay when the source WRITE fails. The at-most-once
  claim must be RELEASED, not burned, so a retry can still deliver the waybill.

## 2. Results

| arm | result |

Arm A reproduced at 559 ms / 6 360 /h on the day's earlier run.

## 3. Why arm C is the arm that matters

Arm B alone would pass against an implementation that stamps the claim
unconditionally. Arm C is the other direction: the write fails, and the
assertion is that **nothing** was recorded - no tracking number, no
`waybillRelayedAt` - so the slot is still open. Only then does the retry
succeed and stamp both.

That is the difference between "the marker exists" and "the marker means
something": a claim burned on a failed relay is a waybill the marketplace never
receives and nothing will ever try to send again. Both directions are asserted
here rather than one.

## 4. Reading the throughput number

6 440/h is the highest per-document figure in this campaign, and the reason is
structural rather than flattering: a label is ONE outbound call the caller
waits on, with no status ladder behind it. Compare the fiscal path (§ F14),
where three quarters of the time is spent asking whether the provider has
finished yet.

## 5. Limits

- Declared 500 ms stub latency, not a real carrier. InPost and DPD publish their
  own rate limits and their own failure taxonomies; neither is measured here.
- n=10 on arm A; arms B and C are single-shot correctness checks, not samples.
- `guard_build` was absent from this scenario before today, so earlier F15
  manifests carry `gitSha: unknown`. This run carries `5897057a`.
