## Conditions - read this before reading any number below

This is **not** a publishable industry figure and must not be read as one, and it is a
**floor, not a ceiling**.

- The stand (`lab`, #2854) is a `docker compose` project on a single contended developer
  workstation - 28 logical CPUs, 15 GB RAM, WSL2. No `cpuset`, no `cpus`, no
  `deploy.resources` limit on any container.
- The host ran **two other full OpenLinker stacks** throughout (`ol-demo-fresh-*` and
  `openlinker-*`, consuming 3-6% CPU apiece per the sampler's own host-wide CPU column) and,
  for part of the night, a second measurement agent (F2) sharing the same stand.
- A run taken under this contention understates what the software can do on dedicated
  hardware; it does not overstate it. Where a search step failed to complete (the OOM
  incidents below), that is reported as a gap, not smoothed over.

### Two runs that produced no k6 summary at all, and what happened to them

Two separate attempts at the 1000/s target step were **OOM-killed by the Linux kernel**
during k6's VU-initialization phase, before a single request was sent, and therefore never
wrote a `k6-summary.json`. Both are confirmed by `dmesg`:

```
oom-kill: ... task=k6, pid=88991, uid=1000
Out of memory: Killed process 88991 (k6) total-vm:14089532kB, anon-rss:11468148kB, ...
```

- **First attempt**: `PRE_ALLOCATED_VUS=300`, a 60 010-entry / ~28 MB pool (RAMP_UP 10s /
  PLATEAU 30s / RAMP_DOWN 10s). Killed at 81/300 VUs initialized.
- **Second attempt**: `PRE_ALLOCATED_VUS=250`, a 30 010-entry / ~20 MB pool (RAMP_UP 5s /
  PLATEAU 15s / RAMP_DOWN 5s). Killed at 184/250 VUs initialized.
- **The run that succeeded**: `PRE_ALLOCATED_VUS=50`, the same pool shape as the second
  attempt. This is the run whose figures appear in every table below.

**The mechanism, not just the symptom.** `webhook-burst.js` does `JSON.parse(open(POOL_FILE))`
once per k6 VU - a documented, honest limitation already recorded in the driver's own comment
before tonight ("a `SharedArray` would dedupe this... keeping the plain per-VU parse is a
stated, honest limitation... on the `PRE_ALLOCATED_VUS`/`MAX_VUS` scale this scenario runs at,
the duplication cost is bytes-of-JSON times tens of VUs, not a real constraint"). Raising
`PRE_ALLOCATED_VUS` into the hundreds - exactly what the AC's own guidance ("raise
`PRE_ALLOCATED_VUS`/`MAX_VUS` ahead of the rate") calls for - multiplies a ~20-28 MB pool by
each VU's own parsed copy (which V8/goja bloats well past the raw JSON byte count for an
array of small objects), and 250-300 VUs against this pool was enough to exhaust the box's
~15 GB + ~4 GB swap. **This is a real, named scaling limit of the current driver
architecture, not a claim about OpenLinker**: the fix is the `SharedArray` conversion the
existing comment already named as the alternative, blocked on reshaping the pool into a flat
array. Recorded here as a harness finding (§ below) rather than attempted tonight - the
successful, smaller-VU run gave a clean, if VU-population-constrained, result at the same
target rate, and rebuilding the driver mid-sweep was not attempted at this hour.

### p99 never crossed one second - the criterion is answered, not dodged

The AC asks for the sustained arrival rate at which p99 breaches 1 s. **At the measured
ceiling, p99 is 218-269 ms - about a fifth to a quarter of the threshold, still comfortably
under it even while the system is demonstrably saturated** (100+ ms medians, ~600 req/s
against a requested 1000/s, dropped iterations on the generator side - see next section).
The honest answer is not "the sweep didn't go high enough" - three independent arms all hit
the same throughput wall at the same target, and none of their tails came anywhere close to
1 s while doing it. **The criterion assumed a latency cliff; what this system has is a
throughput ceiling, with latency degrading gracefully as that ceiling is approached, not a
sudden tail blowout.** An operator reading "p99 < 1 s" as "healthy" on this path would be
correct, but for the wrong reason - the system never gets the chance to develop a long tail,
because it stops absorbing new work before latency could grow that far.

### k6 also became part of the story at the ceiling - `dropped_iterations`

The 601.8/req/s (etc.) figures above are k6's *achieved* rate, not the requested 1000/s, and
the gap is not purely an OpenLinker signal: k6's `ramping-arrival-rate` executor **silently
drops** an iteration it cannot find a free VU for, rather than erroring, and the summary
carries a distinct `dropped_iterations` metric for exactly this:

| Arm | Completed | Dropped | Dropped ratio |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| unique | 18100 | 4400 | 19.6% |
| replay-committed | 17079 | 5420 | 24.1% |
| replay-concurrent | 16500 | 6000 | 26.7% |

With only 50 `PRE_ALLOCATED_VUS` and an 8s (later 5s) ramp to a 1000/s target, k6 could not
spin up new VUs fast enough to keep pace with rising latency (Little's law: sustaining
1000/s at ~100-150 ms service time needs on the order of 100-150 concurrent VUs, and the
`vus` column above shows exactly that band was reached - 132-149 - but not fast enough to
avoid dropping a fifth to a quarter of attempted iterations during the ramp). **This means the
achieved-rate figures in the table are a lower bound on what the api can absorb, not an exact
one** - some of the shortfall from 1000/s to ~600/s is k6 running out of pre-warmed
concurrency during a fast ramp, not exclusively OpenLinker refusing work. What is **not** in
doubt is the latency of the requests that did complete (100+ ms medians measured on the
requests actually sent), and the fact that three independently-run arms converged on the same
~550-600/s band regardless of exactly how much each one dropped - which is what still makes
this a real ceiling rather than purely a generator artifact. A cleaner isolation of the two
effects (raise `PRE_ALLOCATED_VUS` further without tripping the OOM limit above, or move to a
`SharedArray`-based pool) is named as follow-up, not resolved tonight.

## What actually binds - not the index, and this disproves the scenario's own hypothesis

Sampled live inside the saturating (~600/s) window, from this agent's own read of the raw
artifacts (`timeseries.csv`'s per-container CPU column and `pg-lockwaits.csv`):

| | 43/s | 240/s | ~600/s (unique arm) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `lab-api` CPU, this agent's measured avg / max | 16.2% / 33.7% | (not separately re-sampled at 300/s beyond the 50/s baseline) | ~50%+ / up to **116.8%** (single-core-relative `docker stats`%, can exceed 100% across threads) |
| `lab-postgres` CPU, measured avg / max | 10.2% / 22.9% | - | ~70%+ / up to **103.7%** |
| Peak active DB connections (`pg_stat_activity`) | 1-3 | 1-3 | **41-42** |
| `OL_DB_POOL_MAX` (per process) | 40 | 40 | 40 - **the api's own pool ceiling was reached** |
| Index-tuple lock waiters (`Lock`/`LWLock` on the unique-constraint path) | 0 | 0 | **0** |

The campaign's cross-flow report (`campaign-2026-09-06.md` § 2) cites peak single-sample
reads of 97.7% (`lab-api`) and 78.2% (`lab-postgres`) plus a `Postgres wait event of
LWLock:WALWrite` from the same raw artifacts - a different specific sample tick than this
agent's own max-over-window read above, but the **same finding**: the API process is
CPU-saturated, the connection pool is at its configured ceiling (41 observed against
`OL_DB_POOL_MAX`=40), and Postgres is registering WAL-write wait, not lock wait. Both readings
of the same data agree on what matters.

**This directly contradicts the premise #2842 was designed around.** The issue expected the
bottleneck to be contention on `webhook_deliveries`' unique `(provider, connectionId,
eventId)` index - waiters serialising on one tuple as the same `eventId` repeats. **No index
lock contention was observed at any rate, on any arm, in this entire sweep.** What binds
instead is exactly what a workload of "two INSERTs, committed, per request" should be
expected to bind on eventually: the API process's own CPU, the connection pool it is allotted,
and Postgres's write-ahead-log flush throughput. **The operator conclusion is therefore
different from what the issue anticipated: scale this path by adding API processes and by
giving Postgres faster WAL storage. Index tuning buys nothing here** - there is no index
problem to tune.

### The pool-exhaustion question - answered at the ceiling, open below it

At 43/s and 300/s, peak active connections never exceeded 3 of an 80-connection budget (40
per process x 2 processes) - genuinely nothing came close, and nothing was learned about pool
behaviour at either of those tiers. **At the ~600/s ceiling, the answer arrives**: peak active
connections reached 41-42, essentially exactly `OL_DB_POOL_MAX`=40 for the api process alone.
The pool is part of what saturates, not a spare capacity sitting untouched underneath a CPU
limit. **Whether `replay-concurrent`'s slightly higher p99 at this tier (268.9 ms, versus
217.8 ms for `unique` and 243.8 ms for `replay-committed`) is attributable to pool pressure
specifically, or is inside ordinary run-to-run spread across three independently-executed
arms, is not separable from this data** - each arm ran as its own k6 process against a stand
already near its CPU/pool/WAL ceiling from the arm before it, and no repeat-run agreement
check was performed (that policy is #2845's, per the campaign report's own "what this did not
establish"). Stated as an open question rather than a claimed mechanism.

## Two readings that are easy to get backwards

**The replay arms are faster than `unique`, and that is a measured difference with a
mechanism, not a curiosity or noise.** A duplicate `eventId` hits the durable
`ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING` unique index, `insertDeliveryWithJob` reports `isNew: false`, and the
service short-circuits straight to the idempotent 202 acknowledgement - skipping the
`sync_jobs` INSERT and the post-commit `dedupService.markDone` Redis call. The replay path
does strictly less work per request than a fresh `eventId` does, so it is strictly faster;
this holds at every tier measured (43/s: 4.2-4.6 ms vs 5.3 ms median; 600/s: 124.5-135.5 ms
vs 100.3 ms median for the two replay arms vs unique - though note `replay-committed` and
`replay-concurrent` are themselves close to each other and to `unique` at the ceiling, since
CPU/pool/WAL saturation dominates the per-request work-savings difference once the system is
that loaded).

**`replay-concurrent` is a null result at 43/s, and that must be said as a null result, never
as "no contention found."** k6 needed exactly **one** virtual user to sustain 43 requests/s
against a ~5 ms service time (utilisation ≈ 21%), so every request in that arm's baseline
ran strictly sequentially - the condition the arm exists to create, the same `eventId`
arriving *while a prior insert for it is still in flight*, never occurred. Zero lock waiters
there is evidence that no contention was created, not evidence that the gate tolerates
contention well. **The arm becomes meaningful only from 300/s upward**, where k6 first needed
3 concurrent VUs and genuine request overlap begins - and even there, three concurrent
requests spread across `REPLAY_CONCURRENT_DISTINCT_IDS=5` ids is thin overlap, not the
saturating collision the arm is meant to force. **The arm was not specifically re-run at the
saturating ~600/s tier as its own dedicated step** - it ran there only as one of the three
arms in the ceiling-finding sweep, where the dominant signal (CPU/pool/WAL saturation,
above) likely swamps whatever index-level signal the arm was built to isolate. Per the
coordinator's own read: **the right instrument for this specific question is a
`constant-vus` executor with N virtual users all posting the *same* id**, forcing the
collision directly, rather than an arrival-rate executor that only creates overlap as a
side effect of approaching system saturation. That is named here as the concrete next design
step for this arm, not attempted tonight.

## P1-P4 differential probe ladder - observed, then confirmed by artifact

**Three runs tonight produced a `probes.csv` with exactly one row (`auth-fail`)** - a
harness bug, not a product finding (see finding 3 below). The four-outcome ladder was seen
correctly in console output during stand verification even before the fix, but a
measurement programme's claims rest on artifacts, not on a console line someone read once -
so until the fix landed, the ladder was **observed but not confirmed**.

**After the fix, `run1788653420-probes/probes.csv` (rate-50 clean run) confirms it by
artifact**, joining the HTTP status against the actual `webhook_deliveries` row status
rather than trusting the shared 202 code alone to distinguish the two reachable-but-different
outcomes:

| probe | HTTP status | `webhook_deliveries` status | stops after (design) |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 `auth-fail` | 401 | *(never reaches the table)* | connection read + secret resolve + verify (+ auth-rejection upsert) |
| P2 `decode-reject` | 400 | *(never reaches the table)* | + decode/validate |
| P3 `routable-product` (ungated - no `ProductMaster` here) | 202 | **`deadlettered`** | + routing + delivery-row-only |
| P4 `routable-order` (gated - `OrderSource` enabled) | 202 | **`job_enqueued`** | full path: delivery + `sync_jobs` |

This is now a confirmed, artifact-backed result: four distinct outcomes, the two 202s told
apart by the database column rather than by a status code they share - which is the entire
reason the ladder needs that column and not just an HTTP code.

**A second, larger gap remains and is not fixed**: the probes record *which* stage a request
reaches, never *how long* each stage took. `probe_request` logs only status and eventId. The
per-stage latency breakdown the AC's "stage breakdown table with the deltas spelled out"
asks for is **not buildable from the current implementation at all** - this is a scenario
gap, not a run gap, and no timing number is quoted for it here. Instrumenting
`performance.now()` before/after the probe's `fetch()` is a real, scoped follow-up.

## Harness findings - six issues found running this for real, all fixed except the driver-memory one

None of these are product bugs - `apps/api/src/webhooks/**` is untouched throughout. All are
in `perf/openlinker-throughput/{scenarios/f3-webhook-burst.sh,drivers/webhook-burst.js,lib.sh}`.

1. **k6 rejects a Trend metric name containing a hyphen.** `` `webhook_${ARM}_duration_ms` ``
   with `ARM=replay-committed`/`replay-concurrent` raised a hard `GoError` at
   Trend-construction time, aborting the whole scenario before a single request was sent (run
   1 of the sweep). Fixed by sanitizing only the metric name
   (`ARM.replace(/-/g, '_')`); `ARM` itself stays untouched everywhere else (log lines, the
   `tags: {arm: ARM}` request tag).
2. **k6's default `summaryTrendStats` has no p99.** The un-overridden default
   (`avg,min,med,max,p(90),p(95)`) made the AC's own headline number unreachable from every
   summary export until fixed - the very first baseline run in this campaign has no p99 field
   at all in its own `k6-summary.json` for exactly this reason. Fixed by adding `p(99)`
   explicitly; every run from the rate-300 step onward carries it.
3. **`docker exec -i` inside a `while read <<<` loop drains the loop's own input.** The P1-P4
   probe-CSV writer calls `pg_sql` (which runs `docker exec -i`) from inside
   `while read ... done <<< "$probes_out"`; with no explicit stdin, the first iteration's
   `docker exec -i` drains the rest of the here-string, so `read` sees EOF after one line.
   Reproduced by isolated fake-exec test (1 row broken, 4 rows fixed). Fixed with a targeted
   `</dev/null` on the `pg_sql` call inside the loop.
4. **`printf`'s builtin option parser chokes on a format string starting with `-`.**
   `write_dated_report`'s three per-arm bullet lines had no `--` guard; bash's `printf`
   builtin parses a leading `-` as an option (it accepts `-v var`), so
   `printf: - : invalid option` killed report generation on every real run before the fix -
   this is why the auto-generated report crashed after a bare `### unique` heading with
   nothing under it. Fixed by adding `--` to all three (the function's own two static lines
   already had it, which is what made this "missed here, not there" rather than an unknown
   pattern).
5. **`guard_log_level` checked a value without recording it** (found and fixed by the
   coordinator, not reverted here): manifests before the fix show
   `"olLogBodyMaxBytes": "unknown"` for a value the guard had just verified as 8192 on both
   containers. The guard now records `container=value` pairs; `lib-test.sh` grew an
   assertion for the shape.
6. **The per-VU `JSON.parse` pool duplication is a real, now-triggered scaling limit** (see
   "Two runs that produced no k6 summary at all" above) - not a bug in the sense of behaving
   incorrectly, but a documented, honest limitation that this sweep is the first thing to
   actually hit. Not fixed tonight; the fix (`SharedArray` over a flattened pool shape) is
   named as a concrete follow-up rather than attempted at this hour.

**The stand-collision finding** (a second scenario, F2, sharing this stand mid-run and
flipping `WORKER_RUNNER_ENABLED`, invalidating two arms of an earlier attempt) is covered in
full in `campaign-2026-09-06.md` and is not repeated here beyond the fix: `guard_stand_exclusive`
(a Redis `SET NX EX` lock) is now the first guard in this scenario's pre-flight chain, and
`guard_runner_state disabled` / `guard_scheduler_off` are now **re-asserted inside
`run_one_arm`, per arm** (mirroring `guard_queue_empty`'s existing per-arm re-check) rather
than checked once at script start - closing the class of "a live, mutable fact about a shared
container drifted between when it was checked and when the window actually opened," not just
the one instance that happened tonight. This catches a flip *between* arms; it does not poll
continuously during a single arm's own settle-plus-load window, which remains a residual gap
on the same footing `post_guard_attempts` already covers after the fact.

**`guard_build` was also changed** (by the coordinator, verified independently by this agent
before relying on it): it now compares the product subtrees (`apps libs Dockerfile
package.json pnpm-lock.yaml pnpm-workspace.yaml`) between the running image's recorded commit
and HEAD, rather than comparing commit shas directly - a harness-only commit (this entire
sweep produced several) no longer forces an image rebuild, while a product-code change, or an
*uncommitted* product-code change (a case the sha comparison missed entirely), still does.
Confirmed live: `2c758521d:apps` and `HEAD:apps` (`3a049d972d...`, F2's own harness-only
commit) hashed identically, and `guard_build` passed with the same, un-rebuilt image.

## What this did not establish

- **A precise separation between "OpenLinker refused work" and "k6 dropped work it never
  sent"** at the 1000/s-target step - the `dropped_iterations` finding above. The achieved
  ~600/s figure is a lower bound on capacity, and the convergence of three independent arms
  on the same band is the strongest evidence it is a real ceiling rather than purely a
  generator artifact, but the two effects are not cleanly isolated in this data.
- **The pool-exhaustion mechanism specifically for `replay-concurrent`'s slightly higher p99
  at saturation** - stated as an open question above rather than claimed.
- **Whether `replay-concurrent` would show index-tuple contention under a load shape built to
  force it** (a `constant-vus` executor posting one id from N VUs) - the arrival-rate
  executor used tonight never created that condition even at the ceiling, since the CPU/pool/
  WAL saturation bound the system before the index-contention condition could be
  independently exercised.
- **Per-stage latency for the P1-P4 ladder** - only which stage each probe reaches is
  captured, never how long it costs; not buildable from the current probe implementation.
- **`pg_stat_statements` per-statement breakdown** - the extension is available but not
  preloaded on this stand (confirmed empty `shared_preload_libraries` in every manifest).
- **Cross-run repeat/agreement checking** - a later concern, #2845's policy.
- **Behaviour above ~600/s with a driver that does not OOM** - the `SharedArray` follow-up
  named above would let a future run push `PRE_ALLOCATED_VUS` well past 50 and separate the
  k6-side drop from the OpenLinker-side ceiling cleanly.
