# Results D - timing and throughput at 100 000 products (#2644)

Run 2026-08-27/28 on `epic/2590-prestashop-no-module` @ `8e6b2081d`. This asks a different
question from `results-C`: not "did the epic help" but "do its bounds still hold an order
of magnitude above the 10 000 products they were sized against". A regression here is a
sizing finding, not a failure of `results-C`'s comparison, and both stay valid.

## The stand

| | |
|---|---|
| Machine | one host, 28 logical CPUs, 15 GB RAM, Docker on WSL2 |
| **Worker replica count** | **1** (single `ol-perf-worker`, `OL_WORKER_ROLE=jobs`) |
| Lane caps in force | `realtime=4/2 bulk=12/8 fiscal=2/1 fan-out=8/4` |
| Container set | `ol-demo-fresh-{api,postgres,redis,mysql,prestashop,woocommerce,woocommerce-mysql}` + `ol-perf-worker` |
| Network | localhost only, no tunnel, no VPN |

**Lane caps bound one worker PROCESS.** Every cap figure is per process; N replicas
multiply each effective cap by N.

## Honesty ledger for this report

- Figures are labelled **measured**, **derived** (arithmetic on measured values) or
  **extrapolated**. A full-cycle time at this catalogue size cannot be sat through and is
  always derived.
- 10 000-product figures are never restated as if they applied at 100 000.
- A bound that was not reached is reported as **not exercised**, never as holding.
- Store impact is measured with the status-code-checking probe, and this report says so.

## Bound 2 - the two read ceilings (#2608) - ANSWERED, but three of four scans are NOT EXERCISED

`PRESTASHOP_NARROWED_MAX_ROWS = 5000`, `PRESTASHOP_UNNARROWED_MAX_ROWS = 50000`; the read
throws `PrestashopTruncatedReadException` rather than truncating.

Row counts measured in the shop's own MySQL:

| collection | at 10 000 products | read shape | verdict |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `ps_product_attribute` (combinations) | 30 008 | narrowed to 100 products/page (~300 rows) | safe at any size |
| `ps_stock_available` | 40 025 | narrowed per product | safe at any size |
| packs (`cache_is_pack = 1`) | **0** | un-narrowed enumeration | **NOT EXERCISED** |
| `ps_category` | **9** | un-narrowed scan | **NOT EXERCISED** |
| `ps_feature` / `ps_feature_value` | 2 / 10 | un-narrowed scan | **NOT EXERCISED** |
| `ps_attribute_group` / `ps_attribute` | 5 / 30 | un-narrowed scan | definitions only |

Template product 22 has `cache_is_pack = 0`, so the synthetic catalogue mints **no packs at
any size**. The un-narrowed pack enumeration, the category scan and the feature scan are
therefore not exercised by this catalogue and this run says nothing about them. Only a
catalogue with real product variety can.

The combinations prefetch is the one that looked risky and is not: it is narrowed to a page
of 100 products, so ~300 rows, three orders of magnitude under its 50 000 budget.

## Bound 5 - outbox cap and drain (#2604 / #2614) - static answer

| constant | value |
|---|---:|
| `RETENTION_MAX_ROWS` | 100 000 |
| `RETENTION_DELETE_BATCH_SIZE` | 1 000 |
| `RETENTION_MAX_BATCHES_PER_PASS` | 10 -> at most 10 000 deletions per pass |

`backlog_over_cap = ($stillOver && $exhausted)` **cannot fire spuriously by construction**:
it requires the table to be genuinely still over cap AND the pass to have spent all ten
delete batches. An overshoot above 10 000 rows converges over several passes and reports
the flag while it does.

**#2614's AC3 confirmed absent**, which is why that issue is left open: `DeliveryRunner.php`
contains no wall-clock budget of any kind - no deadline, no `microtime` check, no per-run
time limit. The epic's statement about it is accurate.

## Bound 7 - the per-connection sync-status read (#2615) - MEASURED

`GET /v1/connections/:id/sync-status`, five consecutive calls at **33 537** `sync_jobs` rows:

    16.4, 10.9, 8.2, 7.8, 7.9 ms   (all HTTP 200)

The read is two bounded aggregates plus one single-row cursor read: the live-queue aggregate
is restricted to `status IN ('queued','running')` so it scales with the live queue rather
than with history, and the historical aggregate is bounded by a 7-day `createdAt` window.
`averageAttemptDurationMs` correctly reported `null` with `attemptDurationSampleSize: 0`,
because `lastAttemptDurationMs` is NULL on every row predating its migration and the read
excludes NULL rather than counting it as zero.

### Re-measured at the 100 000-product stand

`sync_jobs` held **30 933** rows (23 687 for this connection). Five calls:

    19.6, 9.3, 7.9, 13.0, 7.8 ms   (all HTTP 200)

Unchanged. `averageAttemptDurationMs` now reports **12 729 ms over 178 samples**, so
#2611's column is populated end to end by the epic worker and the read's non-null sample
size is doing its job (12.7 s is a batch child hydrating 100 products, which is the right
order of magnitude).

**Honest limit on this answer.** The history did not actually grow during the campaign -
it fell from 33 537 to 30 933, because every scenario runner purges the connection's
queue. So this measures the read at ~31 000 rows, NOT at "100 000 products with a full job
history", which is what the bound asks about. What can be said from the design rather than
the measurement: the live-queue aggregate is restricted to `status IN ('queued','running')`
and the historical one to a 7-day `createdAt` window, so the cost scales with the live
queue and one week of history rather than with total rows - and `sync_jobs` has no
retention anywhere in the tree, so that bounding is the only thing standing between this
panel and an unbounded scan. **Not exercised at a large history; reported as such.**

## Seeding, and why AC1 cannot be met in one sitting

90 000 products added at `SEED_OFFSET=10000` (ordinals 10001..100000), **in 38 seconds** -
the seed is one set-based `INSERT ... SELECT`, not a row-by-row loop. MySQL went
651 -> 722 MB and did not OOM; the documented exit-137 trap did not fire, and disk was
never a factor (857 GB free, the shop schema was 50 MB at 10 000 products).

| | PrestaShop | OpenLinker |
|---|---:|---:|
| products | **100 000** | 2 878 mapped |
| combinations | 300 008 | 8 526 variants |
| stock rows | 400 025 | |

**#2644's AC1 - 100 000 confirmed on BOTH sides before any figure is taken - could not be
met, and the reason is bound 1 itself.** A full catalogue replication cycle at this size is
~2.8 days (derived). Staging the verification the issue asks for would take days of
unattended running. That is reported as the finding rather than as a setup failure: after
this epic, a 100 000-product shop still takes the better part of three days to replicate
once, and the two sweeps that did NOT get a bigger budget take 10 and 42 days.

Consequence for reading the runs below: a page whose products are already mapped measures
steady state, a page whose products are not measures the identifier-mapping CREATE branch.
Both are legitimate and they are different numbers, so each run states which it was.

## Bound 6 - requests per SKU at 100 000 - MEASURED, and it does not move

| run | elapsed | requests | products | per SKU | mappings created |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 (cold caches, discarded) | 56 s | 35 | 500 | 0.070 | 0 |
| 2 | **46 s** | **29** | 500 | **0.058** | 0 |
| 3 | **46 s** | **29** | 500 | **0.058** | 0 |

Zero retries, every response 200. **Median of runs 2-3: 29 requests, 46 s, 500 products.**

| | 10 000 products | 100 000 products |
|---|---:|---:|
| requests per tick | 29 | **29** |
| elapsed per tick | 46 s | **46 s** |
| requests per SKU | 0.058 | **0.058** |

Measured, and identical to the request. **Requests per SKU does not change with catalogue
size**, because a tick covers a fixed 500 products whatever the catalogue holds - #2593's
budget is per run, not per catalogue. The epic's caches survive the jump: the working set
that matters is one page of 100 products, not the catalogue.

Run 1's extra 6 requests are the cold-start reads (2 `product_options`,
2 `product_option_values`, 1 `configurations`, 1 `currencies/<id>`) after the worker
restart, once per resolver per TTL. Notably that is 2 and 1 rather than the 8 seen in
`results-C` A3: the batch children start staggered enough that fewer of them miss the
cache simultaneously, so the stampede is real but its size depends on arrival timing.

### The late page, and the create branch

`run-a1a.sh` clears the sweep cursor before every run, so on its own it only ever measures
**offset 0**. `run-a1a-at-offset.sh` seeds the cursor to a chosen offset and verifies the
read-back before enqueuing - the first version of that script silently failed to seed
(it wrote a `cursorValue` column that does not exist; the real one is `value`) and
measured offset 0 while claiming offset 99 000. The seed is now verified and a failure is
fatal, because a probe that lies about where it measured is worse than no probe.

| page | requests | elapsed | products | mappings created | proof |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| offset 0, already mapped | 29 | 46 s | 500 | 0 | ids `20..100497` |
| **offset 99 000, unmapped** | **30** | **46 s** | 500 | **500** | ids `208999..209198`, cursor advanced to `:99500` |

Measured. Two things follow, and both are good news for the epic:

**Offset paging does not degrade.** A page 99 000 rows into a 100 000-product catalogue
costs one more request than the first page and exactly the same wall clock. The
`limit=99000,500` read is not a scan the shop struggles with at this size.

**The identifier-mapping create branch is nearly free on the wire.** The late page created
500 new mappings - it took the create path in full - for **+1 request and +0 seconds**.
The create branch is database work, not platform requests, so a first replication costs
the shop essentially the same as a re-sync. That is worth knowing because `results-A`'s
trap list warns that a cold run "takes a different and more expensive code path"; at the
platform boundary, measured here, it does not.

## Bound 3 - the pack-id resolver at 100 000 products - NOT EXERCISED

#2598 enumerates every pack on the connection into a per-connection cache with a 10-minute
TTL. Measured directly against the shop, three times:

    GET /api/products?display=[id]&filter[cache_is_pack]=1&limit=1000
      -> 200, ~30 ms, 132 bytes, ZERO rows, on every attempt

**One request, 30 ms - and that number means nothing about the bound.** The template
product this catalogue is cloned from has `cache_is_pack = 0`, so the synthetic catalogue
contains no packs at any size. The enumeration returns an empty document, so what was
measured is the cost of asking, not the cost of enumerating.

The TTL question is likewise unanswerable from this stand: re-running the enumeration every
10 minutes costs 1 request and 30 ms here, and would cost whatever a real pack set costs
on a shop that has one. **Reported as not exercised.** A shop with a real pack catalogue is
needed to answer it, and this is the clearest case in the study of #2644's own caveat that
"where a bound depends on real product variety (packs, features, attributes), say so".

### What the same probe DID establish: the offset cost of a page read

| read | time |
|---|---:|
| `products?display=full&limit=100` (offset 0) | 254 ms |
| `products?display=full&limit=99000,100` | **386 ms** |

Measured, at 100 000 products. Offset paging costs **+52% on a page read, +0.13 s in
absolute terms**. Across a whole 200-tick cycle that is roughly +26 seconds, against a
cycle measured in days. It is real, it is not free, and it is nowhere near mattering -
which agrees with the request-count result above (30 requests at offset 99 000 against 29
at offset 0).

## Bound 4 - lane caps at 100 000 products: OpenLinker's own queue behaviour

Measured on the running stand, one worker replica, epic defaults
(`bulk=12/8`, `fan-out=8/4`):

| window | created | succeeded | dead | net growth/h |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 h | 537 | 537 | 0 | **0.0** |
| 2 h | 999 | 1000 | 0 | **-0.5** |

**The queue converges.** Drain matches arrival exactly at 100 000 products under the
epic's own lane defaults, and `GET /connections/:id/sync-status` reports `status: idle`,
`arrivalRatePerHour: 348`, `drainRatePerHour: 380`, `alerting: false`.

That is worth putting beside the epic body's "the queue grows ~145 jobs/h faster than it
drains", which this campaign separately measured at ~260/h on PRE-epic code with a
10 000-product catalogue. Same stand, ten times the catalogue, epic code: net zero.

### Per-job-type attempt duration, from #2611's new column

| job type | samples | mean attempt |
|---|---:|---:|
| `master.product.syncBatch` | 80 | 212 031 ms |
| `master.inventory.syncFromSweep` | 700 | 7 555 ms |
| `master.product.syncAll` | 14 | 22 877 ms |
| `master.inventory.syncAll` | 7 | 9 643 ms |
| `marketplace.order.sync` | 2 | 47 178 ms |
| `master.product.syncByExternalId` | 4 | 10 664 ms |

**The `syncBatch` mean must not be quoted bare.** Its 80 samples span two very different
configurations: the runs taken before the missing webservice permission was granted, where
a batch child spent ~1035 s because 996 of its requests were 401s metered out at 60/min,
and the runs after, where a batch child takes ~45 s. The mean is dominated by the former.
The honest figure for a healthy batch child on this stand is **~45 s**, and the column is
doing its job - it is the campaign's own history that is mixed, not the measurement.

`master.inventory.syncFromSweep` at 7.6 s over 700 samples is clean, and is a useful
number in its own right: it is the per-child cost the inventory sweep's budget of 100 has
to fit inside its 15-minute tick, and 100 x 7.6 s = 760 s at perScope 8 means the tick is
comfortably inside its interval.

### Verdict on the caps at this size

The caps do not need to differ at 100 000 products for the reasons this bound asks about:
the queue converges, no lane starves, and per-tick cost is size-invariant. What the caps
CANNOT fix is bound 1 - the number of ticks. Raising `bulk` further would not shorten a
2.8-day cycle, because the sweep is bounded by its per-run budget and its cron, not by how
many children run at once. **Recommendation therefore stays with bound 1's budgets, not
with the lane caps.**

One measured caveat carried over from `results-C`: at `bulk` perScope 8 the Redis rate
limiter timed out 85 times in one window and entered degraded mode, where pacing falls back
to a PER-PROCESS insurance limiter. With one replica that held the shop at 60/min. With N
replicas a degraded episode would pace per process, so shop-side rate could approach N x
the declared limit. **Not exercised - this run used one replica.**

## Bound 8 - store impact at 100 000, with the status-checking probe

Same method as `results-C` A2: three phases, `SWEEP_PAGE_LIMIT=2000`, `PHASE_SECS=120`,
jobs-only worker, and the probe that counts only 2xx into the percentiles and refuses a
ratio over a window that recorded any error.

| phase | samples | median | p95 | max | errors |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| idle 1 | 219 | 0.041 s | 0.049 s | 0.198 s | **0** |
| under sweep | 215 | 0.041 s | **0.072 s** | 0.509 s | **0** |
| idle 2 | 219 | 0.040 s | 0.054 s | 0.086 s | **0** |

    p95_idle_mean = 0.0514 s
    p95_under_sweep = 0.0721 s
    RATIO_B_over_A = 1.403

### This is the finding: store impact does NOT scale the way request count does

| catalogue | p95 ratio under sweep | requests per tick |
|---|---:|---:|
| 10 000 products | **1.053** | 29 |
| 100 000 products | **1.403** | 29 |

Measured, both with zero errors in every window. **The same 29 requests hurt the shop
eight times as much at ten times the catalogue.**

The mechanism is visible in this study's own page-read probe: request COUNT per tick is
identical at both sizes, but each remaining request is a heavier query -
`products?display=full&limit=100` costs 254 ms at offset 0 and 386 ms at offset 99 000 on
this catalogue - and the storefront shares MySQL with those reads. Tail latency moves with
it: the load phase's max is 0.509 s against 0.198 s idle.

So the two halves of the epic's store-impact story scale differently, and only one of them
was ever measured at scale:

- **request count per SKU is size-invariant** - confirmed here, 0.058 at both sizes
- **store impact is not** - 5.3% at 10 000 products, 40% at 100 000

Neither figure contradicts the epic; both were taken on its code. What they do is bound the
claim. "p95 under a full catalogue sweep is 0.989x idle. The shop does not slow down" was
measured on a 10 000-product catalogue with a probe that did not check status codes. With
the probe fixed it is 1.053 there, and 1.403 at the size this study exists to test.

A 40% p95 increase is still a shop that works - 72 ms against 51 ms - and it lasts only
while a tick runs. It is not an outage. It is simply no longer "does not move", and an
operator sizing a much larger catalogue should expect the trend to continue rather than
flatten.

## Bound 5 - the outbox burst was NOT generated, and why

`seed-products.sh` writes rows straight into MySQL. It never runs PHP, so no PrestaShop
hook fires and **no outbox row is created** - the outbox held 4 rows before the seed and
4 after. Generating the burst this bound is about would mean driving ~100 000 product
writes through the webservice so the module's hooks run, which is a campaign of its own
and would take far longer than the seed it followed.

What can be stated without it, from the constants and the code:

| | |
|---|---:|
| `RETENTION_MAX_ROWS` | 100 000 |
| `RETENTION_DELETE_BATCH_SIZE` | 1 000 |
| `RETENTION_MAX_BATCHES_PER_PASS` | 10 (so <= 10 000 deletions per pass) |
| `backlog_over_cap` | `($stillOver && $exhausted)` |

`backlog_over_cap` **cannot fire spuriously by construction**: it requires the table to be
genuinely still over cap AND the pass to have spent all ten delete batches. An overshoot
above 10 000 rows converges over several passes and reports the flag while it does, which
is the intended signal rather than a false one.

**#2614's AC3 confirmed absent** - `DeliveryRunner.php` contains no wall-clock budget of
any kind: no deadline, no `microtime` check, no per-run time limit. The epic's own
statement about it is accurate, and it is why that issue is correctly left open.

**Verdict: not exercised.** Whether the queue converges under a real 100 000-row burst is
unanswered by this run.

## Summary - the eight bounds

| # | Bound | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | sweep budget and real cycle time | **Needs a change.** Derived: 2.8 days (catalogue), 10.4 days (inventory), 41.7 days (deletion reconcile). #2593 raised one budget and left two. |
| 2 | the two read ceilings | **Holds where exercised.** Nothing approaches 5 000 or 50 000. Three of four un-narrowed scans are NOT exercised by a synthetic catalogue. |
| 3 | pack-id resolver | **Not exercised.** No packs at any size; 1 request, 30 ms, zero rows. |
| 4 | lane caps | **No change needed at this size.** Queue converges, net 0.0/h. What hurts is the tick COUNT, which caps cannot fix. |
| 5 | outbox cap and drain | **Not exercised.** Seeding fires no hooks. Static analysis only; `backlog_over_cap` cannot fire spuriously. |
| 6 | requests per SKU | **Holds.** 0.058 per SKU at both 10 000 and 100 000 - identical to the request. |
| 7 | per-connection sync-status read | **Holds at 31 000 rows** (8-20 ms). Not exercised at a large job history. |
| 8 | store impact, fixed probe | **Degrades with size.** p95 ratio 1.053 at 10 000, **1.403 at 100 000**, zero errors in all six windows. |

### The one recommendation, with its evidence

**Raise the budgets of `master.inventory.syncAll` and `master.product.reconcile`, or
shorten their cadence.** Proposed, not applied, per this issue's own acceptance criteria.

Evidence: a catalogue-sweep tick now costs 29 requests and 46 seconds against a 20-minute
interval - about 4% of its window - so there is a great deal of headroom in the tick, and
the binding constraint is the number of ticks a cycle needs. The inventory sweep's own
per-child cost is measured at 7.6 s over 700 samples, so a budget of 100 fills 760 s of a
900 s tick at perScope 8; that one is closer to its ceiling and should be raised together
with its cadence rather than alone. The deletion reconcile has the most headroom and the
worst consequence when it is slow (#1689's pause never fires, and a deleted product keeps
selling), so it is the first one to move.

### What this study did NOT establish

- The OpenLinker side never reached 100 000 products (2 878 -> 3 378 mapped). A full
  replication cycle is ~2.8 days; #2644's AC1 is not satisfiable in one sitting.
- Bounds 3 and 5 were not exercised at all, for the reasons above.
- Bound 7 was not exercised at a large job history.
- Every figure is single-replica. Lane caps bound one PROCESS; N replicas multiply them.
- The synthetic catalogue has no packs, 9 categories and 2 features, so anything depending
  on real product variety is untested.
